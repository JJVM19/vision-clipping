Songs that blew up because of edits: 12 cases and what artists can copy

October 2026 · Vision Clipping · Cited case studies, 2022 to 2026
Summary
  • Edits break songs in six repeatable ways: sped-up versions, TV and film fan edits, networks of edit pages, memes, paid clipping campaigns and sport. Every case below fits one of them.
  • The common thread is repetition under content people already wanted. RAYE’s “Escapism” reached UK number one after sped-up remixes soundtracked over 700,000 TikTok videos.
  • None of these songs needed people to care about the artist first. The edit did that work, and the official sound turned viewers into listeners.

Every year a handful of songs arrive in the charts by a side door. Not radio, not a playlist, but a sped-up remix, a fan edit of a TV scene, a meme or a run of football videos. Here are twelve of them, grouped by the kind of edit that carried them, with the numbers from the people who measured them.

1. Sped-up versions

RAYE ft. 070 Shake, “Escapism.” Fans sped the song up before anyone at a label did. TikTok says multiple sped-up remixes amassed over 700,000 video creations, RAYE released official sped-up versions to meet the demand, and nearly three months after release the song went to UK number one with 5.6 million streams in its chart week. It was her first number one, as an independent artist. NME counted over 47 million Spotify streams for the official sped-up version alone.

Miguel, “Sure Thing”. A 2010 song that had peaked at No. 36 came back thirteen years later when a sped-up edit took over TikTok. Billboard reported the sped-up version soundtracked more than 4 million videos, and the song set a record for weeks on its R&B and hip-hop chart while climbing to No. 15 on the Hot 100, a new peak.

Sexyy Red and Tay Keith, “Pound Town”. A portion of the original was used in over 140,000 TikTok videos, and Billboard credits the gains to multiple versions running at once: the original, sped-up, slowed-down, instrumental and a bass-boosted mix. The Nicki Minaj remix gave Sexyy Red her first Hot 100 entry.

The lesson from all three: every version of a song is another way into an edit. Sped-up, slowed, instrumental and bass-boosted cuts cost almost nothing to make and multiply the places a track can live.

2. Fan edits of TV and film

Lady Gaga, “Bloody Mary”. A 2011 album track reached a new UK peak of No. 22 in January 2023. The Official Charts Company put it plainly: the song took advantage of a deluge of TikToks using it to soundtrack fan edits of the dance scene from Netflix’s Wednesday.

Kate Bush, “Running Up That Hill”. After Stranger Things used the song, it was blowing up on TikTok and became Kate Bush’s second UK number one, 37 years after release. It finished as the UK’s song of the summer with 86.6 million streams over the period. The sync opened the door; the edits kept it open for months.

3. Networks of edit pages and fan pages

Kordhell and the rise of phonk. Drift phonk spread through TikTok pages posting car racing and weightlifting edits. Black 17 Media set out to build exclusive relationships with those pages, and told Billboard that when it signed Kordhell he was doing 5,000 plays a day; he went on to 4 million a day on Spotify alone.

bbno$, “It Boy”. His manager told Billboard the team had close to 60 fan pages posting one to four times a day, some anime edits, some clips of old podcasts, all ending on the song. It peaked at No. 10 on Billboard’s TikTok Top 50, and by March 2026 had 142 million Spotify streams.

4. Sounds that turn into a format

Jordan Adetunji, “KEHLANI”. The Belfast artist’s official sound had been used in more than 287,000 clips, with another version in over 315,000, when the song entered the Hot 100 at No. 70 in July 2024.

Jess Glynne, “Hold My Hand”. A 2015 song became the soundtrack to the “Nothing beats a Jet2 holiday” meme. It was used over nine million times, with videos viewed over 80 billion times, and TikTok named it its UK Song of the Year for 2025. Nobody planned the meme, but the format (travel fails, holiday mishaps) gave millions of people a reason to use the sound.

5. Paid clipping campaigns

John Summit, “Lights Go Out”. This is the cleanest public example of a paid clipping campaign working. Variety reported an eight day campaign in January with a $1,050 budget that produced 32.4 million views across 29 clips. The top clip alone did 5.3 million, and the best performers put the song behind Steph Curry and Michael Jordan highlights: a basketball edit, not an advert.

Justin Bieber, “Beauty and a Beat”. During Coachella, paid clipping campaigns for his performances ran openly, and his catalogue drew 664 million streams in a week, up 171%. “Beauty and a Beat” climbed to No. 1 on the Billboard Global 200. Vulture is careful that the lift cannot be split cleanly between the shows and the clips, and so are we. What the case shows is that the biggest names now run clipping around their biggest moments.

6. Football

Oasis, “Wonderwall”. In summer 2026 the song became the unofficial soundtrack to England’s World Cup. TikTok reports a 6,800% surge in searches and 336,000 creations, and the Official Charts show it climbing back to No. 2 on the chart of 23 July. TikTok’s own summary of the summer: “If the dancefloor provided one soundtrack to summer 2026, football provided another.”

What artists can copy from these songs

  • Give the song more than one shape. RAYE, Miguel and Sexyy Red all broke on versions, not just the original. Release sped-up, slowed and instrumental cuts early.
  • Attach the song to something people already love. A TV scene, a goal, a basketball highlight, a holiday meme. The edit carries the attention; the song rides it.
  • Use many pages, not one. bbno$ had around 60. Phonk labels worked with the pages behind car and gym edits. Repetition across many feeds is what makes a hook familiar.
  • Post for weeks. “Escapism” took nearly three months to reach number one. Most of these songs built over weeks, not days.
  • Always link the official sound. Every one of these runs counted creations on the sound page. That number is the one to watch.

Some of these songs got lucky with a meme or a sync. The rest were helped on purpose: pages, versions and campaigns built to keep the song in front of people. Frequently asked questions

Which songs went viral because of edits?

Well documented examples include RAYE’s “Escapism” (sped-up remixes, UK number one), Miguel’s “Sure Thing” (sped-up edit, new Hot 100 peak), Lady Gaga’s “Bloody Mary” (Wednesday fan edits), bbno$’s “It Boy” (around 60 fan pages) and Oasis’s “Wonderwall” (World Cup football videos, UK No. 2 in 2026).

Why do sped-up songs go viral on TikTok?

Sped-up versions fit short videos: the hook arrives faster, the energy is higher, and they suit edits and dance clips. Labels now release official sped-up versions because fan-made ones kept charting songs, as with RAYE’s “Escapism”.

Can a paid clipping campaign make a song go viral?

It can put a song in front of tens of millions of people quickly. Variety reported a $1,050 campaign for John Summit’s “Lights Go Out” producing 32.4 million views from 29 clips. Whether the song then catches depends on the hook and on keeping it in feeds for weeks.

How do I get my song into edits?

Release versions editors want (sped-up, slowed, instrumental), make sure the official sound is live, seed edit pages in formats that suit the song, and keep posting for weeks. Some artists run prizes for the best edit; others work with a clipping operation that posts edits daily across its own pages.
