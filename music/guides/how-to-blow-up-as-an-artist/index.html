How to blow up as an artist in 2026: the clipping playbook

October 2026 · Vision Clipping · For independent artists, rappers and labels
Summary
  • Releasing is not the moment any more. 106,000 new tracks reached streaming services every day in 2025, and 88% of all tracks were streamed fewer than 1,000 times all year.
  • Songs break where people already watch. TikTok says all 13 UK number ones in 2025 had a viral moment on the app first, and labels now treat clipping as a standard part of a campaign.
  • The playbook: find the ten seconds that hit, set up the sound properly, put the song under edits people already watch, post every day across many pages, and keep going for weeks.

The old way to blow up as an artist was a good release and a lucky playlist. In 2026 the release is just the start line. The songs that travel are the ones people hear twenty times under football edits, culture recaps and meme comps before they ever search the artist’s name. This is how that works, and how to make it happen on purpose.

Why releasing a song is not enough in 2026

The numbers are brutal. Luminate’s 2025 year-end report, as covered by Music Ally, counted around 106,000 new tracks delivered to streaming services every day, and found that 88% of available tracks were streamed fewer than 1,000 times in the whole year. Uploading a song puts it on the shelf. It does not put it in front of anyone.

That is the gap every artist has to close: not making the music, but getting it heard by new people, repeatedly, until it sticks.

Where songs actually break now

Short form video is where that happens. A TikTok-commissioned Luminate study found that 84% of songs that entered the Billboard Global 200 in 2024 went viral on TikTok first. In the UK, TikTok reports that all 13 Official Chart number ones in 2025 had a viral moment on the app. Those are the platform’s own figures, and Billboard is careful to note that TikTok activity does not count towards the charts directly. But the pattern holds across every year of data: the songs that chart are the songs that were already everywhere in people’s feeds.

The industry has noticed. In March, Variety reported that clipping, paying editors to post snackable content set to an artist’s song across many accounts, has become such an integral part of music promotion that many major and independent record companies now offer it as a standard tool. bbno$’s manager put it more bluntly to the same reporter: “I don’t know anybody not utilizing [clipping] who’s actually competitive in the marketplace.”

The playbook, step by step

1. Find the ten seconds that hit

Nobody hears your whole song in an edit. They hear one hook, one drop, one bar, usually under ten seconds. Before spending anything, listen to your track the way a scroller will: which ten seconds would make someone stop on a goal replay or a fight clip? If you cannot find them, no amount of posting will fix it. Pick the strongest section and build everything around it.

2. Set up the sound properly

Make sure your distributor has delivered the track to TikTok and Instagram, and that the official sound shows your name and artwork. Every post that uses the song should be linked to that official sound, not a re-upload. The sound page is where views turn into fans: TikTok says its Add to Music App button has been used to save over 3 billion songs to streaming services. A clip with your song on someone else’s upload is reach you cannot collect.

3. Put the song under content people already watch

This is the part most artists miss. Your own posts ask people to care about you. An edit asks nothing: it shows them a goal, a knockout, a viral moment or a top five list, and your song happens to be playing. The music gets heard by people who would never have tapped on an artist’s page.

Labels have been doing this quietly for years. Billboard reported that the team behind bbno$’s “It Boy” had close to 60 fan pages posting one to four times a day, and the song peaked at No. 10 on Billboard’s TikTok chart. In phonk, Black 17 Media built relationships with the edit pages behind car and gym videos; the label told Billboard one of its artists went from 5,000 plays a day to 4 million a day on Spotify. And in January, an eight day clipping campaign for John Summit’s “Lights Go Out” put the song behind Steph Curry and Michael Jordan highlights: 32.4 million views from 29 clips on a $1,050 budget, according to Variety.

4. Post every day, on many pages, for weeks

One great edit is a lottery ticket. Thirty edits a week across a dozen pages is a system. Repetition is what makes a hook familiar, and familiarity is what makes people go looking for a song. Pick two or three formats where your track fits (football, news and culture, ranked lists, dance or meme comps) and keep the song in them every day for at least a month before judging it.

5. Give fans and creators something to make

The best campaigns turn viewers into posters. Nemzzz did it directly, offering a cash prize on his own TikTok for the best football edit to his new single. RAYE did it by following the crowd: after fan-made sped-up versions of “Escapism” spread across TikTok, she released official ones, and the song went to UK number one as an independent release. Sped-up, slowed and instrumental versions are cheap to make and give editors more to work with.

6. Watch the right numbers

Views on a clip are the start, not the result. The numbers that tell you a song is catching are the ones further down the loop: how many videos use your sound, how many saves the track gets, and whether daily streams are climbing week on week. TikTok’s study found that artists whose streams tracked their TikTok activity grew 11% week on week, against 3% for everyone else. If views are high and sound usage is flat, the edits are entertaining but the hook is not landing; change the section of the song, not the budget.

Mistakes that stall a song

  • Buying fake engagement. Bought views and bot plays do nothing for discovery and can get accounts flagged. Every view should be a real person on a real device.
  • One big influencer post and nothing after. A single post is one moment. Without daily follow-up the song is gone by the weekend.
  • Posting without the official sound. Views on an unlinked upload never reach your sound page, your profile or your streams.
  • Switching songs every week. Familiarity takes repetition. Give one song a month before moving on.
  • Hiding paid posts from creators. In the US, undisclosed endorsements can cost more than $50,000 per violation under the FTC rule adopted in late 2024. Edits on pages that are open about what they are avoid the problem.

Frequently asked questions

How do you blow up as an independent artist in 2026?

Find the ten seconds of your song that hit hardest, make sure the official sound is live on TikTok and Instagram, then put the song under short edits people already watch (football, culture, comps) and post them every day across many pages for at least a month. Track sound usage, saves and daily streams, not just views.

How do you blow up as a rapper on TikTok?

Rap travels best under edits with energy: football, fight and culture clips, and ranked lists. Give editors a clean hook, sped-up or instrumental versions, and a reason to post, and keep your song in those formats daily. Nemzzz, for example, offered a prize on his own TikTok for the best football edit to his single.

How long does it take for a song to blow up?

There is no fixed timeline, but daily posting needs weeks to build familiarity. RAYE’s “Escapism” reached UK number one nearly three months after release, once sped-up edits had spread. Judge a push after a month, not a week.

Do I need a label to blow up?

No. RAYE’s first UK number one was an independent release, and the tools labels use (edit pages, clipping, creator seeding) are available to independent artists. What a label adds is budget and volume, which a clipping operation can also provide.

How much does it cost to promote a song?

It ranges widely. Billboard reported creator posts from $25 to about $10,000 each, and Variety put typical music clipping campaigns at $1,000 to $5,000. Our managed packages start at £1,000 a month for 90 posts. See our full guide to song promotion costs.
