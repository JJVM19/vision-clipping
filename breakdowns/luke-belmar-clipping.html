Luke Belmar Stopped Posting in 2023. The Clipping Machine Didn’t.

August 2026 · Vision Clipping · A cited breakdown of the most rented audience in the creator economy
Summary
  • Belmar has uploaded ten YouTube videos in his life. The last, in September 2023, was titled “The end of phase one”. His official TikTok has 13,800 followers. He is still everywhere.
  • The reach lives in other people’s channels: a 2.7M-view Simon Squibb interview, a 1.3M-view THRST episode, and a swarm of fan clip pages, the biggest archive channel holding 1,100+ videos.
  • The launch that machine powered: Capital Club, 15,000+ members in 73 countries. Whop credits clipping for the blow-up, and we know because part of that machine was ours: 200M+ views and $19M in subscriptions from two owned pages.

Luke Belmar has uploaded ten YouTube videos in his life. The last one, posted in September 2023, was titled “The end of phase one”. He has posted nothing there since, his verified TikTok has 13,800 followers, and yet he remains one of the most-clipped men on the business internet, with 723,000 followers on X and roughly 700,000 on Instagram. Iman Gadzhi built a machine and bought the marketplace it runs on. Belmar is the opposite experiment: what happens when the machine is almost entirely other people.

The dormant channel

The numbers on Belmar’s owned platforms read like an abandoned storefront attached to a packed stadium. His YouTube channel has about 450,000 subscribers and ten uploads, the biggest a 3.9M-view visit to the Tate brothers from 2022, and nothing new in almost three years. His TikTok is a rounding error in the economy built around him. Meanwhile his X account, where he actually posts, carries 723,000 followers and aphorisms like “Money flows where attention goes. Connect the dots.”

He has connected them. In an interview with Jack Neel, Belmar argued that distribution decides what gets seen, that it has finally become economically viable for individuals, and that marketing content matters more than making it. His own platforms are proof by omission: the attention arrives without him uploading anything.

Reach that lives in other people’s channels

Where does it come from? First, the podcast circuit. Simon Squibb’s Belmar episode has done 2.7 million views; his breakout First Things THRST appearance sits at 1.3 million, with a follow-up near 600,000; and the bookings continue into 2026. Single guest appearances now out-view his entire channel catalogue.

Second, the swarm. A fan-run archive channel holds 1,100+ videos for 97,000 subscribers; another has amassed 1,500 videos since 2023; Instagram fan pages with names like Belmar Society appear, from public profile data, to collectively rival his main account’s following. Every podcast appearance becomes raw material within hours, cut into hooks by pages he does not run and, in most cases, cannot even identify.

Capital Club: the launch the swarm paid for

All that borrowed reach pointed somewhere: Capital Club, Belmar’s private entrepreneur network, which today claims 15,000+ members across 73 countries. The clipping marketplace Whop, in its own marketing, says Belmar “used this same clipping system to blow up his personal brand, making tens of millions on his launch of Capital Club”. Belmar himself has never publicly detailed a bounty programme, so treat that as the platform’s claim. What is public is what professional clipping did for the launch, because part of it was ours.

Forbes credited our co-founder Emrah Bayraktar with personally generating over 400 million views for brands including Iman Gadzhi and Luke Belmar. Our Belmar case is on this site: two pages built from zero to 80K Instagram and 150K TikTok followers, 200M+ views, and $19 million in Capital Club subscriptions driven through distribution. Emrah’s line in that same Forbes piece doubles as the manual: “the brands getting the highest ROI treat clips like performance media - they test which content actually drives clicks, signups, or sales, then double down on what converts.”

The catch in a fully rented machine

Belmar’s model is the purest demonstration that clips are the content, and it comes with the purest version of the trade-off. He owns almost none of it. The archive channels can go dark, pivot to the next guru, or misquote him into a controversy, and there is nothing to stop them; the biggest “Luke Belmar” clip channel on YouTube writes its description in the first person and links his real accounts, and there is no way to tell whether it is his. The swarm chooses the narrative. The swarm also chooses the audience, which means nobody is steering the views toward the people who might actually buy anything.

That is the difference between reach that happens to you and reach that works for you. The owned layer of Belmar’s launch, the pages built deliberately, aimed at buyers, measured against subscriptions, is the part that converted attention into $19 million of memberships. Views are not created equal. The part of Belmar’s reach that turned into $19 million of memberships was the part somebody aimed. The same lesson, from the opposite end of the spectrum, is in the Iman Gadzhi breakdown.

Frequently asked questions

Does Luke Belmar have a YouTube channel?

Yes, but it is effectively dormant: about 450,000 subscribers, only ten videos ever uploaded, and nothing new since September 2023. Single podcast appearances about him now out-view his entire channel catalogue, and his reach is carried by third-party shows and fan-run clip pages.

How did Capital Club grow so fast?

Through clip-driven distribution rather than owned posting. Whop’s own blog credits Belmar with using its clipping system to blow up his personal brand for the Capital Club launch, and our public case study reports 200M+ views on two owned pages driving $19M in Capital Club subscriptions.

Who runs the Luke Belmar clip pages?

Mostly fans and anonymous operators. The largest archive channel has close to 100,000 subscribers and over a thousand videos, and the biggest Instagram fan pages appear to rival his own account’s following. Almost none of them are verifiably operated by Belmar, which is exactly the trade-off of a fully rented audience.
